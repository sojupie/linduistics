# First-principles exploration for a public language-data project

This is an exploration, not a proposal for a replacement taxonomy, schema, source, or site. Earlier experiments remain available as optional stress tests; they do not define the new project's subject or scope.

## Purpose and decision still open

Start from the person and the question they want to answer. Do not start from the old database, its five-language set, routes, visualizations, categories, or the fact that Wiktionary is already processed. None of those is the product specification.

The project should keep the work that makes it personally worthwhile: finding sources, extracting and transforming data, and making the result useful to someone beyond its author. It should not become a thin interface over a finished corpus. Reusing e xisting data is compatible with that goal when the useful work is in linking, checking, enriching, or making it easier to explore and reuse.

The product question is not settled. Possible people include a curious reader following a word across languages, a language learner comparing forms and meanings, someone investigating how languages differ or influence each other, and a data maker trying to turn messy sources into reusable material. These are examples only. The project may serve a different person, combine several jobs, or prove that one small tool or dataset is enough.

Do not pick a subject because it matches the old app, is easiest to extract, has a familiar label, or appears novel. For each candidate job, define the question, the useful result, what the person can do with it, what data and transformations are needed, and what already available sources can contribute. An existing tool is a comparison or an input—not a novelty gate. A direction is promising if it helps a real person, produces an inspectable and reusable result, can be regenerated from traceable inputs, and keeps the sourcing/transformation work enjoyable. It does not need to be academically novel or broadly important.

## Ground-up functional requirements

These requirements describe a useful public dataset, regardless of which linguistic subject it covers:

- **A clear job:** State the real question or task the data supports. “Explore language” is too broad on its own.
- **Units that fit the question:** Decide what counts as an item only after defining the question. It might be a language, a grammatical feature, a word sense, a form, a text example, a historical claim, or a relation among them.
- **Evidence and transformation are inspectable:** A user can trace a published value to its source record and understand what the project extracted, linked, inferred, or calculated.
- **Scope and gaps are visible:** Users can tell what languages, time periods, concepts, and record types are covered, and where data is missing or uneven.
- **Multiple views do not erase distinctions:** One item may have several sources, meanings, histories, features, or forms. A display label must not overwrite the underlying assertions.
- **Useful without this app:** Provide documented, downloadable files and stable identifiers so someone can query or transform the data independently. A website may make discovery easier, but it is not a substitute for a usable release.
- **Repeatable releases:** Pin source versions and transformation code; publish versioned outputs and a short account of meaningful changes.
- **Rights are workable:** Check the actual terms of every source before redistributing source text or derived material.

These are not requirements to build a large graph platform, exhaustive coverage, formal certainty scores, or a full research apparatus. A narrow dataset can satisfy them.

## Candidate questions, not a fixed taxonomy

Depending on the chosen job, useful questions might include: what does this form mean in context; how do two languages express a selected concept; which grammatical features do two varieties share; what evidence connects two word forms; or how does a borrowing path cross language families? Do not require one dataset to answer all of them.

For any chosen question, ask what exact unit the answer refers to, whether the result is an observation or a source's assertion, how it was derived, and what a reader can inspect or do next. Missing and conflicting information should remain legible without demanding numerical certainty scores.

## Separate kinds of data when a question needs them

There is no master taxonomy to settle before choosing the project. Genealogical classifications, typological feature values, areal/contact evidence, lexical meanings, source claims, and calculated form comparisons answer different questions. They can be linked when that helps a user, but do not combine them merely because they all concern language.

The invariant is simpler: preserve who or what made a statement, what the statement applies to, and how the project derived its own results. A language-level family claim should not silently become a word-origin claim; a dictionary's borrowing note should not silently become the project's conclusion; a similarity measurement should not silently become evidence of common ancestry. If competing records disagree or leave a gap, expose that distinction in a form appropriate to the chosen task. A number called “confidence” is not required.

Different project directions would need different units. A typology release might need language varieties, features, values, definitions, and references. A lexical exploration might need source-specific entries, senses, forms, and links. A corpus-derived tool might need texts, tokenization choices, and reproducible measurements. Those are examples, not a union schema to build now.

The resources divide along the questions they were built to answer; none is a ready-made version of the whole project:

### Earlier candidate only: a source-to-structure evaluation set

The current search suggests a possible narrow contribution: a small set of real source entries that lets a data maker check whether an extraction preserves what the source actually says and which entry, etymology, sense, or component it applies to. This is not a set of claims that a word's history is correct. Its test target is whether tools preserve source structure without merging homographs, discarding competing etymologies, turning components into whole-word claims, or confusing a borrowing link with inherited descent.

The potential user is someone building or checking lexical-history data from Wiktionary or another semi-structured dictionary. The output could pair a stable source revision/locator with parsed records and a brief explanation of the hard cases. It could help its author and possibly another data maker compare extractors or understand source structure. Its small scale is acceptable if that use is valuable; the practical test is whether the output actually saves effort or enables a check that the user wants to perform. This is one candidate, not the default. Prior parser trouble alone does not show that anyone needs the artifact.

Start each proposal from five questions, before choosing categories, sources, or a schema:

1. Who would use this result, even if only a few people?
2. What do they want to find out or make?
3. What answer, file, or interaction would let them do that?
4. What data and transformation work is required to produce it?
5. What existing source or tool already does the same job, and what remains difficult?

This keeps the exploration open without making it directionless. A feature, taxonomy, language set, or source is relevant only if it helps answer one of those questions.

Do not assume that “connecting more scales” is itself the answer. Genealogy, typology, areal contact, lexical history, and present-day similarity answer different questions. Put them together only when a concrete task needs more than one. A useful test compares a task using one data domain with a task that needs links across domains; if the links add no insight, keep the data separate.

Areal data is available, but it is not one ready-made global layer. AUTOTYP explicitly tries to define areas without using the linguistic patterns that researchers may later test against those areas; ATLAs instead develops regional feature sets to maximize areal signal, and UraTyp focuses on Uralic variation. This is a useful design distinction: a project can store an externally defined contact area, a language's observed feature value, and a computed spatial pattern as different evidence types. It should not derive a contact edge from map distance and present that edge as history. [AUTOTYP data model](https://github.com/autotyp/autotyp-data/blob/master/datasets.md) · [ATLAs methods and data](https://atlas.evolvinglanguage.ch/) · [UraTyp 2.0](https://github.com/cldf-datasets/uratyp) · [Grambank coverage](https://grambank.clld.org/)

The source set itself is evidence against one universal taxonomy. Loanword histories, shared ancestry, language-family classifications, typological values, and same-form/multiple-meaning observations have different units and are supported by different evidence. A useful dataset can connect them, but should not collapse them into one relation field or one `cognate / false cognate / other` partition. [IDS](https://ids.clld.org/) · [WOLD](https://wold.clld.org/language) · [CLICS⁴](https://clics.clld.org/) · [WALS downloads](https://wals.info/download) · [Glottolog downloads](https://glottolog.org/meta/downloads) · [D-PLACE sources and license](https://d-place.org/source) · [Lexibank](https://lexibank.clld.org/)

### One possible build pattern: a versioned bridge dataset

If the tested user job needs several existing sources to work together, the project does not need to absorb every upstream dataset into a new master database. A **versioned bridge dataset** could point to upstream records and publish the mappings and transformations the project actually created. This is one build pattern, not the product decision; a task that requires new observations or a focused collection should use that instead.

1. Pin each upstream release by citation, version/DOI, license, retrieval date, and checksum. Keep a resource's original IDs and don't silently refresh its records when upstream changes.
2. Link varieties and concepts to stable external identifiers when the match is clear. Store the source ID, mapping decision, and alternatives when it is not. Do not treat matching names or spellings as proof of identity.
3. Keep extracted source statements, source entries, and the project's own computed observations in separate records. A similarity score, feature match, and etymological claim are different evidence types.
4. Publish project-created mappings, selected case records, transformation code, and derived indexes. Reuse an upstream record by citation/ID when license or duplication makes redistributing its text inappropriate.
5. Release ordinary readable files first. Use CLDF modules where they fit a real wordlist, dictionary, or structure dataset; use CLDF Generic or a separate bridge table for project-specific links instead of forcing unlike records into a Wordlist. CLDF explicitly supports Generic datasets and interoperability across multiple modules. [CLDF modules and components](https://github.com/cldf/cldf/blob/master/README.md)

This gives the project a real contribution even when it does not create all source observations from scratch: transparent cross-source links, tested extraction and normalization, and queryable derived results. It also permits a later site to answer new questions without treating its first endpoint design as the data model.

NorthEuraLex, Concepticon, IE-CoR, and CLDF are options, not commitments. The first sample should use whichever sources best answer its chosen questions. CLDF is a useful public interchange format: its Wordlist model can represent forms, cognate assignments, borrowing links, and partial cognacy. It does not determine the internal database design or make a claim correct. [CLDF Wordlist](https://github.com/cldf/cldf/blob/master/modules/Wordlist/README.md) · [NorthEuraLex](https://github.com/lexibank/northeuralex) · [Concepticon](https://concepticon.clld.org/about) · [IE-CoR](https://github.com/lexibank/iecor)

### Promising inputs, not a predetermined foundation

NorthEuraLex is a particularly practical candidate to inspect early: its CLDF release has 1,016 concept prompts and 121,611 lexical forms across 107 varieties, and its language list includes English, German, Swedish, Danish, and Norwegian Bokmål. The release declares CC BY 4.0. It can supply a reproducible comparison frame and a useful baseline for checking coverage and transformations. It is not a complete dictionary, does not answer every historical question, and should not dictate the language scope. The CLDF conversion notes that it substantially adjusted the source IPA, so any sound analysis must retain that transformation history and validate the relevant forms. [NorthEuraLex CLDF release and license](https://github.com/lexibank/northeuralex) · [NorthEuraLex language list](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lexibank/northeuralex/master/cldf/languages.csv) · [NorthEuraLex metadata](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lexibank/northeuralex/master/cldf/cldf-metadata.json)

IE-CoR is a contrasting reference: it uses 170 precisely defined meanings, specialist-selected lexemes, and cited cognate judgments. It is useful for testing whether extracted or computed results agree with documented historical analysis on aligned forms. It is limited to its sampled meanings and languages, and its expert judgments are not a substitute for the project's own sourced transformations. [IE-CoR methods and license](https://iecor.clld.org/)

### What the current local artifacts actually demonstrate

The existing work is a useful prototype, not yet a public dataset. The source-linked diagnostic export contains 15 deliberately selected case groups, 30 pair comparisons, 179 entries, 677 senses, and 1,586 extracted source-claim records. It preserves ambiguous source identities and candidate links rather than resolving them silently. The separate source-evidence slice contains 21 pinned page revisions, 70 structured assertions, 10 pair reviews, and 3 supporting-source observations. The eight-concept sense worksheet has 47 local candidates, all marked provisional. These counts show that extraction and evidence preservation are feasible, but they do not show that the interpretations are correct, representative, or ready to publish. The CLDF export has not had the official validator run, and its selected Wiktionary material has not been reviewed entry by entry for redistribution rights. [Case-slice README](../datasets/cldf-case-evidence/README.md) · [Case-slice manifest](../datasets/cldf-case-evidence/manifest.json) · [Source-evidence README](../datasets/source-evidence/README.md) · [IE-CoR sense candidates](data/iecor_local_sense_candidates_2026-09-29.tsv)

**Salvage judgment:** carry forward the source pages, explicit locators, extraction experiments, and difficult cases as inputs and regression material. Do not carry forward the live database's pair labels, vocabulary-wide interpretation of mined candidate counts, or existing category distribution as the dataset's results. The diagnostic slice shows that source claims can be retained at entry/component scope and that ambiguous matches can stay unresolved in a reproducible export; it does not prove the pair histories, represent a language's vocabulary, establish public redistribution rights, or establish that a global etymology dataset is the right product. Rebuild the deliverable around the user job that survives comparison with existing resources.

A separate local check mapped IE-CoR's forms to the five current Wiktionary-derived language inventories: exact spelling matched all five local inventories for 159 of 169 complete meaning slots. IE-CoR's own selected forms share a cognate-set ID in 134/159 Swedish–Danish slots and 141/159 Danish–Bokmål slots. These are bounded counts over a defined meaning sample, not corrections to the application's mined pair percentages and not Linduistics judgments. The remaining sense mapping, alternative forms, and phrase cases still need inspection before this can be called a reviewed slice. Detailed joins and limits are in [the salvage research notes](DATASET_SALVAGE_RESEARCH.md).

The local *bank* case shows how much meaning and etymology can be lost when records are reduced to a single comparison label. Four mined comparisons are all labeled legacy `other`, although they include exact modern forms with strong phonetic similarity and selected meanings ranging from river edge to financial institution and storage. The source records preserve distinct English etymology sections: the financial sense is traced through French and Italian to Proto-West Germanic `*banki` / Proto-Germanic `*bankiz`, while the river-edge sense is traced through Old English to `*bankō` / `*bankô`. The Swedish source entry selected for a river-edge sense has one entry-level etymology section naming Dutch, German, or Low German as routes and then `*banki` / `*bankiz`; the current export does not establish whether that entry-level claim applies to the selected sense. So `other` hides the questions that matter, but this slice does not yet justify a final river-bank relationship either. Keep the source paths and sense scope visible, and mark the cross-sense connection unresolved until the source data supports it. [Local comparison and claim records](../datasets/cldf-case-evidence/comparisons.csv) · [English Wiktionary *bank*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/bank) · [Source-slice README](../datasets/cldf-case-evidence/README.md)

### Public reuse is a source-selection constraint

Track license and attribution at the source-record level. Wikimedia text is generally available under CC BY-SA 4.0 and GFDL, with attribution and share-alike requirements; individual entries can include material with separate terms, so check the page and its history before redistributing copied text. Bokmålsordboka and Nynorskordboka make their content available for download and reuse under stated terms; the Norsk Ordbank inflection data is separately identified as CC BY 4.0. A combined export cannot assume one license just because its tables share a format. [Wikimedia reuse terms](https://foundation.wikimedia.org/wiki/Terms_of_Use) · [Ordbøkene open data](https://ordbokene.no/about/open-data)

The source check found a useful alternative to scraping the Danish dictionary: DanNet publishes downloadable RDF, CSV, and WN-LMF data under CC BY-SA 4.0, and links many of its senses to Den Danske Ordbog. The DDO's own copyright page reserves text and data mining rights and says other copying needs a prior written agreement. Use DanNet as its own licensed lexical-semantic resource; do not treat its links as permission to copy DDO content. [DanNet downloads and license](https://wordnet.dk/dannet/page/downloads) · [DDO copyright terms](https://ordnet.dk/copyright/)

Sense and morphology resources exist for many languages, but they do not arrive as one complete multilingual dataset. For example, SALDO is a Swedish lexical-semantic and morphological lexicon published under CC BY 4.0; WordNet-SALDO links Swedish senses to Core WordNet and is also CC BY 4.0. Open German WordNet is CC BY-SA 4.0, and the Norwegian Bokmål WordNet is listed under CC BY 4.0. OMW 1.4 lists 4,476 Danish synsets, 4,455 Bokmål synsets, and 6,796 Swedish synsets; its documentation warns that the Danish and Norwegian coverage is only a small subset and that the interface is focused on links to Princeton WordNet. OMW 1.4 does not list OdeNet. OMW 2 aims to link independent wordnets through CILI instead, but those links still need validation. These are examples of source coverage and licensing differences, not a proposed language set. [OMW 1.4 coverage and limits](https://omwn.org/omw1.html) · [OMW 2 and CILI](https://omwn.org/omw2.html) · [SALDO](https://spraakbanken.se/en/resources/saldo) · [WordNet-SALDO](https://spraakbanken.se/en/resources/wordnet-saldo-2-0) · [OdeNet](https://github.com/hdaSprachtechnologie/odenet) · [Norwegian WordNet metadata](https://data.norge.no/en/datasets/f479d8ff-03aa-3af5-a8d5-03854a9ba190/norwegian-wordnet-bokmal)

## Possible methods for lexical questions (conditional)

- Keep project comparison prompts separate from WordNet synsets and other external concept IDs. English-led WordNet mappings are useful evidence, but wordnets can split, merge, or lexicalize meanings differently. Store each external mapping as a sourced link to the project's prompt; do not make it the canonical meaning identity. Test CILI/OMW 2 as one bridge, not as an assumed solution.
- Use spelling and pronunciation similarity to retrieve candidates, not to decide ancestry.
- Treat orthographic matching and phonetic alignment as different tools. A simple spelling distance can find lookalikes; sequence methods such as LexStat model recurring sound correspondences across a language pair. Run them only on comparable meanings with traceable pronunciation/transcription data, and treat output as a candidate ranking rather than a verdict. [LexStat paper](https://aclanthology.org/W12-0216/) · [LingPy method documentation](https://lingpy.org/docu/compare/lexstat.html)
- Use meaning prompts to create comparable slots. Record which source senses were selected and where the match is uncertain or plural.
- Use sound-correspondence or cognate-detection methods to propose groups only on meaning-aligned forms with known transcription provenance. Compare methods against documented references on those references' own forms before making claims about performance.
- Extract source etymology claims without dropping direction, multiple targets, or their scope. Keep claims that conflict; do not force them into one answer.
- Treat component links as component links. A shared part of a compound can be historically related even when the whole compounds do not have the same history.
- Define misleading-similarity candidates through explicit current-form and meaning criteria. Keep them queryable even when origin is unknown.
- Compare any automated cognate proposals with independently documented judgments only after matching their exact forms and meanings. Report where the methods disagree; do not tune until they reproduce every reference judgment or transfer a benchmark's judgments wholesale.
- Keep source pronunciations separate from generated pronunciations. A populated IPA field is not evidence that the source supplied IPA.

## A source-to-release workflow (apply only after choosing a task)

This is a general release path, not a plan to process Wiktionary or build a new app:

1. **State the user's question and useful output.** Define what the output should let someone inspect, compare, or make.
2. **Choose one small real source slice.** Record release/revision, source IDs, retrieval date, license, and why the records answer the question. Do not assume an API or dataset is complete because it is easy to download.
3. **Preserve source-native records.** Keep the source's own units and identifiers. Do not merge entries or values just because names, spellings, or codes match.
4. **Make transformations explicit.** Store project-created links, normalized values, model outputs, and computed measurements separately from the source. Pin method and version so the result can be regenerated.
5. **Test the result at the right level.** Use manually checked examples, a genuinely independent reference, or comparison with source-native output as appropriate. State which cases and failure modes the test covers; don't generalize a tiny check into a broad accuracy claim.
6. **Publish the smallest useful artifact and recipe.** Include files, metadata, source attribution, limitations, and the command or steps to regenerate it. Add a website only if it makes the tested task materially easier.

Start with readable files. Use CLDF if one of its modules or its generic format helps another person consume the result; use another format if that fits better. The format follows the data and task, not the reverse.
Wikidata Lexemes is worth testing as one possible source for step 3: it assigns separate identifiers to lexemes, senses, and forms, and its model permits statements at those levels. Its coverage must be measured against the actual scope: the Wikidata dashboard compares its forms to corpora and currently reports different form and token coverage rates for Swedish and Danish. High token coverage does not mean the resource contains most word types or the senses needed for a particular question. [Wikidata Lexeme model](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Lexicographical_data/Documentation/en) · [Wikidata coverage dashboard](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Lexicographical_coverage/en)

CLDF should remain an exchange layer, not the theory of the dataset. It has established `Wordlist`, `Dictionary`, `StructureDataset`, and `Generic` modules; wordlists can attach cognate judgments and borrowing data, including partial cognacy. If the central output is a new kind of sourced claim graph, use the closest standard components and document the extra tables rather than contorting the claims into an unsuitable label column. [CLDF modules and design](https://cldf.clld.org/) · [CLDF Wordlist module](https://github.com/cldf/cldf/blob/master/modules/Wordlist/README.md) · [CLDF terms](https://cldf.clld.org/v1.0/terms.html)

For a richer lexical graph, OntoLex-Lemon separates lexical entries, forms, senses, and the source dictionary's entry structure. Its LemonEty extension models etymologies as hypotheses, supports more than one hypothesis per entry, and can express ordered paths or graph-shaped histories. A 2026 Gallo-Sicilian dataset uses it to encode 10,193 lexical entries and borrowing paths with sound changes. This is a useful prior model, not a reason to choose RDF or a claim of novelty. OntoLex's lexicography module is a community specification, not a W3C standard; LemonEty is an extension vocabulary, not part of CLDF's standard tables. Start with CLDF/CSV if ordinary tabular files meet the actual reuse need; add RDF only if a tested consumer needs traversable lexical graphs. [OntoLex lexicography module](https://www.w3.org/2019/09/lexicog/) · [LemonEty proposal](https://doi.org/10.3390/info9120304) · [Gallo-Sicilian dataset paper](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4176/swodch-6.pdf)

## Choosing a first vertical slice

Do not start by auditing the old rows, picking a taxonomy, designing a universal schema, or building a website. First compare a few sharply stated questions from different parts of linguistics. The examples below are prompts to investigate, not a product menu:

| Question someone might bring | Possible data work | What would make the result useful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “How do these languages express this idea?” | Gather aligned forms, senses, examples, and pronunciation across a defined set of concepts. | A person can compare real usage and inspect coverage and source entries. |
| “How does this word change when its grammatical role changes?” | Keep lemma, form, and feature-bundle records together; compare or generate forms only for a selected task. | The user can inspect real forms and distinguish source-listed forms from generated analyses. |
| “How does this language differ from its neighbors?” | Link sourced structural observations to named language varieties and a map or family context. | The selection of features, varieties, and missing values is transparent and reusable. |
| “How do speakers build this kind of sentence?” | Compare real annotated examples or parallel text, retaining corpus, genre, and annotation provenance. | The user can inspect actual examples and tell corpus observations from language-wide generalizations. |
| “How did this word or feature spread?” | Trace sourced changes or contact evidence at the level the source supports. | A reader can inspect the path and distinguish a recorded claim from a project's interpretation. |
| “Can I make this difficult source useful as data?” | Extract a versioned source slice and publish a repeatable transformation with known failure cases. | Another person can reuse the output or pipeline without adopting this project's app. |

These directions have different trade-offs; do not merge them preemptively:

| Direction | Useful starting resources | Interesting project work | Main practical constraint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Compare forms for shared concepts | NorthEuraLex, IDS, Wiktionary | Meaning alignment, phonetic/semantic comparisons, inspectable sampling | A bounded prompt list is not a whole-language vocabulary; external prompts and word senses may not align cleanly. |
| Follow a word's recorded history | Wiktionary/Wiktextract, language-specific dictionaries, WOLD | Extract and connect source claims, variants, components, loans, and conflicting paths | Source statements differ in granularity and rights; extraction may be substantial. |
| Compare grammatical features or contact | Grambank, WALS, AUTOTYP, regional resources | Join feature values, classifications, sources, and geography into a focused comparison | Feature definitions, areal boundaries, and coverage vary; maps alone do not explain why a pattern exists. |
| Compare sentence patterns in use | Universal Dependencies treebanks and selected parallel corpora | Reproducible parsing, sampling, and comparison of constructions in actual text | Corpora differ by genre, size, and annotation; parsed frequencies are about those texts, not every speaker. |
| Publish a source-transformation tool or dataset | Depends on the source and task; CLDF can package several data shapes | Make one difficult source or transformation understandable and reusable | The audience may be small, so the artifact needs a clear job and a low-cost release path. |

This is a shortlist to test, not a ranking. A single useful direction can be enough; links between directions should be added only when the chosen task needs them.

### What real records let a person do

The examples below test different kinds of language questions. They are not proposed product requirements. They show why source selection must follow the question, and why rehosting a dataset is not enough by itself.

| Question a person could ask | Real data example | What the source already lets them do | What a new project would need to add | Current reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Where does a demonstrative go relative to a noun in a particular language? | Grambank's Angaataha entry for demonstrative order gives the example `kɨmandɨ utɨpɨhandɨ` (“this thin book”), with the demonstrative before the noun and a cited grammar source. | Grambank defines the feature, records a value, gives an example, and links its source. | A different task, audience, or example-based workflow that the existing feature page does not provide. A map of the same values would add little. | A good test of sourced structural data; a weak case for copying a broad typology database. [Grambank GB025](https://grambank.clld.org/parameters/GB025) |
| How is a construction expressed in a translation? | LinES gives the pair “Did you see the elephants?” → “Såg ni elefanterna?” with word alignments. It shows English `did` plus subject before the verb, versus Swedish verb-first order. | The original resource already includes aligned sentences and grammatical annotation. Its stated purpose included studying how constructions and words translate. | A concrete reader workflow, such as finding comparable question patterns and inspecting how their parts map. Current UD files pair sentence segments, but word alignments require a separate request; the Swedish feature annotations were assigned by a program and not manually checked. | Promising only if the transformed view makes a specific task easier. A new corpus browser alone repeats the source. [LinES paper](https://aclanthology.org/W07-2441.pdf) · [English LinES](https://universaldependencies.org/treebanks/en_lines/index.html) · [Swedish LinES](https://universaldependencies.org/treebanks/sv_lines/index.html) |
| What grammatical information is packed into a word form? | UniMorph 4.0 tags English `drinks` as `V;PRS;NOM(3,SG)` and shows a Georgian verb with `V;FUT;NOM(1,PL);ACC(2,SG)`. The latter encodes future tense plus first-person plural nominative and second-person singular accusative arguments. | It already provides inflection tables and a feature system intended to compare morphology across languages. Its 2022 paper reports 182 languages in the release; the current index and repository totals differ, so versions must be pinned. | A bounded task such as showing how a form encodes its grammatical roles, checking attested forms against a re-inflection tool, or identifying which paradigms are missing. The source and feature conventions vary across language datasets. | A practical data/NLP task, but only a new transformation, validation, or learning workflow would distinguish it from rehosting. [UniMorph 4.0 paper](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.89.pdf) · [UniMorph index](https://unimorph.github.io/) · [Swedish repository](https://github.com/unimorph/swe) |
| What evidence connects two words, and what other relations coexist? | The current Linduistics candidate pool mixes translation links and surface-similarity matches; the local source-claim extraction preserves some Wiktionary claims. IE-CoR supplies a separate meaning-aligned cognacy sample for the five current varieties. | The current app can retrieve interesting pairs; IE-CoR can serve as a bounded comparison source for its own chosen forms and meanings. | Separate units for form, sense, component, and attributed source claim; a defined concept sample for any rate; and views that keep inheritance, borrowing, derivation, and similarity distinct. | This is the closest salvage path if word relations remain interesting, but it must be rebuilt around a defined question and sample. It cannot turn the current candidate pool into a vocabulary-wide statistic. See [the refresh retrospective](PROJECT_POSTMORTEM_AND_LEARNINGS.md) and [source-salvage research](DATASET_SALVAGE_RESEARCH.md). |

These examples do not yet identify a winning direction. They establish a useful decision rule: build only when a real question exposes a gap that the source does not already answer. The gap may be in the data, the transformation, or the way a person can inspect and reuse the result. “It is a dataset we can download” is not enough.

### What can be salvaged without inheriting the old design

The old work is useful as raw material, not as the foundation for a new model:

- **Keep as optional inputs:** extracted source records and identifiers; parser and candidate-retrieval code; pronunciation, morphology, and re-inflection tools; named edge cases; source snapshots and transformation notes.
- **Keep only for its narrow job:** the current comparison pool can help find potentially interesting or misleadingly similar pairs. Its row counts describe the pipeline's candidates, not language vocabulary or the prevalence of a historical relation.
- **Do not carry forward as requirements:** the five-language scope, one row per spelling, one exclusive category per pair, the current `other` bucket, its current percentages, the current database schema, or any old page layout.
- **Rebuild only what a tested question needs:** a bounded sample, a source crosswalk, a morphology set, a typology slice, or a corpus transformation. Do not build a universal schema before one of these samples demonstrates a need for it.

The current extraction manifest records 710,110 source-entry records and 978,265 source-sense records across the five language partitions. English accounts for 536,081 entry records; Swedish 49,105; Danish 22,013; German 78,534; and Norwegian Bokmål 24,377. These are records extracted from language-specific slices of English Wiktionary, not independent native-edition sources, sampled word lists, or measures of vocabulary coverage. The counts show there is substantial raw material to reuse; they do not show that the records are comparable or that the derived categories are correct. See the [source review](DATASET_SALVAGE_RESEARCH.md) and the local manifest `pipeline/data/etymology_records_rebuild_20260929.jsonl.manifest.json`.

The current six-case JSONL fixture is a useful salvage test, not a representative dataset. It contains 36 candidate comparisons, 22 case-specific source-entry records, 10 pair-claim matches, and 7 component links. A read-only inspection shows what these extracted records can and cannot support:

| Case | What the source records say | What the fixture preserves | What remains unresolved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *fleece* | The Swedish entry says it was borrowed from English. The English entry traces its own history through Middle English and Old English to Proto-West Germanic `*fleus`. | Separate source entries and claims: Swedish `borrowed_from English`; English `inherited_from` ancestors. | The entries describe two steps in a path. They should not be collapsed into a claim that Swedish inherited the word independently from Proto-West Germanic. |
| *bankman* | The Swedish entry says `bank + man`. | A `compound_of` claim and one component-entry candidate. | The component candidate is not a resolved link to the intended senses or a claim about the full compound's ancestry. |
| *clown* | The Danish, German, Bokmål, and Swedish entries say their forms were borrowed from English. The English entry describes the word as likely North Germanic in origin. | Multiple source entries and directed claims, including claims that can form a longer path. | These are Wiktionary-authored claims from one source family, not independent confirmation. The path must retain its source, direction, and scope. |

This is the real salvage opportunity: the extraction can expose interesting paths and components that one display category hides. It does not yet resolve all entry-to-sense links, reconcile source disagreements, or provide a sampling frame. The fixture is too small and too deliberately selected to estimate accuracy or prevalence. Its source is Wiktionary via Kaikki/Wiktextract; it is not an independent linguistic audit.

### Existing NLP service: useful prototype, not a product constraint

The service is another candidate asset, but its current implementation is narrower than its endpoint names suggest:

| Code path | What it currently does | Consequence for a fresh direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `/analyze` in [`nlp_service.py`](../pipeline/src/nlp_service.py) | Loads an installed spaCy model for a language. If no model is available, it falls back to a blank tokenizer with a sentence splitter. | Dependency and morphology output depend on an installed model; the fallback does not perform a syntactic parse. |
| `/inflect` | Looks up stored forms in `word_inflections` and ranks them by overlap with a finite mapping from spaCy features to Wiktionary tags. | This is form retrieval and ranking, not a general inflection generator. Its useful coverage depends on database forms and compatible tags. |
| `/translate-approximate` and `/pan-germanic` | Choose a target word for each input token from `word_comparisons`, favoring current category labels, semantic score, and part of speech; then try to retrieve an inflected target form. | This is a word-substitution baseline. It does not do sentence-level translation or reorder the syntax. It inherits the candidate table's limits and should not define a new data product. |

These paths may still be useful tools to test against a chosen task. Their names and presence in the repository are not evidence that a task is solved or that the next dataset should be built around them.

There is also a reproducibility gap: the tracked pipeline dependency files do not declare FastAPI, spaCy, or any language model packages. The service may rely on a separate environment, but that runtime setup is not captured by the pipeline's declared dependencies.

For word-history questions, the most practical starting shape is not a replacement `category` enum. Preserve the source entry and sense identities; store each source-authored relation as its own attributed claim; store project-generated measurements and links separately; and let a word have multiple claims at different scopes. For other questions, use their natural units instead. A typology value is about a language variety and feature; a corpus observation is about text; an inflected form is about a lemma and grammatical bundle. Link these domains only when a tested task needs the link.

### Concrete candidate from the broader source search: translation patterns

Universal Dependencies has matching English and Swedish LinES treebanks. Each segment in one is paired with a translation in the other, with material from literature, online manuals, and Europarl. The treebanks report 5,696 Swedish sentences and 102,538 tokens; both pages list CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. The task they enable is concrete: **“How is this construction expressed in the translation?”** A small derivative could let a reader find examples by a syntactic pattern and compare the translated structure, with the source pair and annotations visible. [English LinES description](https://universaldependencies.org/treebanks/en_lines/index.html) · [Swedish LinES description and statistics](https://universaldependencies.org/treebanks/sv_lines/index.html)

This is not a clean gold-standard dataset ready to relabel. The English annotations were converted to UD and partially reviewed; the Swedish morphology features were assigned by a program and not manually checked. The UD GitHub README says segments are word-aligned but directs users to contact the maintainer to obtain those alignments; the downloadable treebank files are CoNLL-U, so token links are not obviously part of the release. A prototype would need to obtain the alignment file or derive alignments, then manually check a small sample. Both halves are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. The original LinES project already studied how common constructions and function words are translated, so a new result would need to make a specific workflow or transformed output useful; merely placing the corpus in a new viewer adds little. [UD English LinES README](https://github.com/UniversalDependencies/UD_English-LinES) · [UD annotation and licensing notes](https://universaldependencies.org/contributing/licensing.html) · [original LinES purpose](https://www.ida.liu.se/~larah03/transmap/Corpus/)

For each prompt, make one tiny example using real material and answer five things: who is it for; what can they do after seeing the result; what source records and transformations produce it; what existing resource already helps; and is the sourcing/transformation work worth continuing? Compare usefulness, clarity, traceability, reproducibility, practical coverage, reuse rights, and personal interest. Do not score “novelty” as a requirement. Reuse another resource if that best serves the user.

Select one question only after comparing the examples. Then define the narrowest data slice that answers it, document the transformations, and see whether a downloadable release alone is useful. Adopt CLDF or another standard when it solves an actual interoperability need. Add a site only when it materially improves discovery or use of the data. Keep old examples such as *Ombudsmann* or *kuningas* only if they happen to test the selected question; their existing investigation is not a reason to pick that question. Do not touch the live app database as part of this exploration.
## What can carry forward

Reuse old artifacts only when they help answer the new questions:

- Raw Wiktionary-derived records, source IDs, and sense IDs can be useful inputs.
- Existing pronunciation, morphology, re-inflection, semantic, and pair-retrieval code can be evaluated as transformations.
- Existing named examples can become test cases if they still expose important structures.
- Existing external-source checks can inform resource selection.

Do not preserve the old one-category-per-pair model, its candidate-pool denominator, lemma-only identity assumptions, the current schema, or earlier worksheets as requirements. Replace or discard them if the new slice shows they block the intended uses.

## Decision gate

Choose a direction when a real-data example produces an output that the intended person can use, understand, and reproduce from documented inputs and transformations—and when making it is a project the author still wants to work on. The output need not be unprecedented or comprehensive. Record its coverage and limits in terms that match what was actually sampled.

After that, expand only when the tested job shows what is missing: more records, another source, a new level of linguistic description, or an added transformation. Do not add a domain because it is adjacent, or widen the scope before the first release proves useful.

- CLDF models wordlists and cognacy relations, including partial cognacy and borrowing, as linked data: [Wordlist module](https://github.com/cldf/cldf/blob/master/modules/Wordlist/README.md), [CLDF terms](https://cldf.clld.org/v1.0/terms.html).

- Wikimedia text reuse requires license compliance and attribution; Ordbøkene describes its download and reuse terms and identifies Norsk Ordbank's CC BY 4.0 license: [Wikimedia Terms of Use](https://foundation.wikimedia.org/wiki/Terms_of_Use), [Ordbøkene open data](https://ordbokene.no/about/open-data).
- Separate WordNet-style resources illustrate the difficulty of assembling cross-language sense links: OMW 1.4 omits German and documents Danish and Norwegian as partial PWN-linked subsets. OMW 2 aims to connect independent wordnets through CILI: [OMW 1.4 coverage and limits](https://omwn.org/omw1.html), [OMW 2](https://omwn.org/omw2.html), [OdeNet](https://github.com/hdaSprachtechnologie/odenet), [DanNet](https://wordnet.dk/dannet/page/downloads), [Norwegian WordNet](https://data.norge.no/en/datasets/f479d8ff-03aa-3af5-a8d5-03854a9ba190/norwegian-wordnet-bokmal), and [WordNet-SALDO](https://spraakbanken.se/en/resources/wordnet-saldo-2-0).
- CILI is an interlingual index for wordnets, not a guaranteed meaning gold standard. Its maintainers describe the integration and integrity problems across wordnets: [CILI repository](https://github.com/globalwordnet/cili), [Some Issues with Building a Multilingual Wordnet](https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.390/).
- DDO's current terms reserve text and data mining rights and require written permission for other copying: [copyright page](https://ordnet.dk/copyright/). DanNet is a separate downloadable resource under CC BY-SA 4.0: [downloads](https://wordnet.dk/dannet/page/downloads).
- Concepticon links concepts defined in published concept lists; it is not a multilingual lexicon: [about Concepticon](https://concepticon.clld.org/about).
- IE-CoR publishes selected forms and cognate sets, and its processing distinguishes loans, parallel derivation, and other cases: [dataset](https://github.com/lexibank/iecor), [loan handling](https://github.com/lexibank/iecor/blob/master/iecorcommands/make_nexus.py).
- Tatoeba provides downloadable sentence pairs under CC BY 2.0 FR, while OPUS aggregates corpora whose terms and sources vary by collection: [Tatoeba downloads](https://tatoeba.org/en/downloads), [OPUS corpus catalogue](https://opus.nlpl.eu/corpora).
- LexStat is a published method for automatic cognate proposals from multilingual wordlists; its output depends on comparable forms and transcription, and remains an analysis to evaluate rather than ground truth: [paper](https://aclanthology.org/W12-0216/), [LingPy documentation](https://lingpy.org/docu/compare/lexstat.html).
- Wiktionary extraction works over human-oriented wiki markup. Wiktextract documents one approach to producing structured dictionary data: [paper](https://aclanthology.org/2022.lrec-1.140/), [Wiktionary parsing guidance](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Wiktionary:Parsing).
